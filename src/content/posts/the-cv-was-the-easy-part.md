In my [last post](/posts/i-rebuilt-my-cv-as-code/) I turned my CV into a build pipeline, which felt like a serious solution to a serious problem. It was a good project. It also solved about 10% of the actual problem.

The other 90% is what happens after a human (or something that passes for one) reads the CV. Here is what that looked like over two and a half months in Germany: more than a hundred applications, over sixty interviews, a pile of coding challenges, a few assessments with no human attached, some in-person days, and one interview with a chatbot.

I'm still deciding between offers, so this is not a victory lap. It's a field report. No company names, no recruiter names, and every quote is paraphrased. The stories are real.

## Table of contents

## The job description as fiction

One role at a big consultancy was titled something like Cloud Engineering Manager / Architect. Reading the requirements, I counted the following jobs in one person:

- customer-facing architect
- hands-on coder (there was a three-hour coding challenge to prove it)
- expert in cloud, networking, Kubernetes and AI
- project manager
- team lead, on the project and possibly beyond it
- the person responsible for the commercial success of the projects, which meant carrying a serious share of the company's profit target
- traveler, at least half the time

During the first two interviews I asked what they were looking for. They said a person to join the company, not a project. In a later interview a director told me there was no project right now, which is why I needed one more interview. I did not fully follow the logic, and I'm fairly sure nobody else did either. I ended up with five rounds in total, the last of which was two directors grilling me on technical questions. The director never looked at my coding challenge, so he couldn't say how well I program. My answers on the business side were "vague". People inside the company, who had worked with me before, gave me good feedback, which did not seem to matter.

Then came the offer. It was well below what that list of jobs would suggest. They told me they couldn't go higher, since paying me more would be unfair to others in the same role. The sweetener they could add was a one-time signing bonus, worth about the price of a nice laptop.

That's an architect, a developer, a project manager, a team lead and a sales target, for one salary and a laptop. Beau-ti-ful. I did not accept this opportunity.

Another company was looking for a technical network engineer with leadership responsibilities, Hamburg only, at a salary that would have been fair for half of that description. I told the recruiter this would be a hard role to fill. She agreed, and asked whether she could use my CV as a template for the search. I said yes. It's the only time my CV has been a requirements document, and I like to think I contributed.

## Location roulette

One US cloud giant listed a role as "Berlin, Frankfurt, Hamburg or Munich". I live in Frankfurt. The recruiter's first question was when I was moving to Munich. The role, it turned out, was Munich only. Fine.

She moved me to a similar role with the same four cities. The new recruiter asked when I was relocating to Berlin. That role was Berlin only. Fine again.

I got a third role, again with a referral. This time the recruiter confirmed that Frankfurt was fine. I did the phone screen, built a presentation, and sent my availability for the full interview loop. In the first interview the hiring manager explained that I would need to be in Munich, or travel there once a week. That's a four-hour drive. I have driven it in my head several times and I don't like it.

I finished the loop anyway. The feedback was that I was not quite right for the level I applied for, but good for the one below. The recruiter said they would look for a role at that level for me, then disappeared from the face of the Earth.

## The assessment zoo

I learned that "assessment" is a genre, and its entries have very little in common.

- **The email grading test.** 90 minutes, three parts. In the first, you read a customer email and then rate five different replies from "highly inefficient" to "highly efficient". There are 20 emails, and you can't go back to change an earlier grade. The second part is a technical quiz. The third shows you two unrelated statements, one on the left and one on the right, and asks which is more like you. I still don't know what I told them about myself.
- **The three-hour coding challenge, before any human contact.** The company hadn't spoken to me yet, but it felt strongly that I should spend an afternoon on its problem. Three weeks later I got an automated rejection, which at least matched the process.
- **The 15-minute architecture case.** A 30-minute slot with two interviewers: 5 minutes of introductions, 5 minutes for questions at the end, 5 minutes to read the case study, and 15 minutes to answer it. The case study described a company with 40 agents, a prompt-injection incident, tripled AI costs, an acquired subsidiary with a legacy monolith, a 12-month migration deadline, and a peak of 50,000 concurrent users. I spent my time asking clarifying questions. I'm still not sure whether that was the right strategy, but it was the only one I had.
- **The "probation day".** A day in an office in another city, unpaid, which turned out to be three interviews in a trenchcoat.

## The interview with a chatbot

I also went through a fully AI-led interview: a chatbot with a voice, asking me questions and listening to my answers. I answered politely, as one does.

Then nothing. No rejection and no next step. I have been ghosted by something that I assumed could not forget me, which is a new low even for the genre. I still wonder whether it was a screening process, a training exercise, or just a way to collect data about how candidates describe their experience. I will never know. If it was a screening, I'd like to hear what I did wrong, but I'm not sure whom to ask.

## Calendars are hard

Several companies had problems with the simple act of meeting me.

- A recruiter at one company didn't join the first call. I wrote a polite email. Eight hours later I got a short apology and a new slot. Fine, things happen. The screening call was held by the hiring manager himself, which tells me something about staffing.
- A recruiter at another company also didn't join the call. I wrote a polite email. Nothing on Friday, nothing on Monday. On Tuesday I got an automated rejection saying my skills and experience didn't match what they were looking for. I'm impressed by how fast the machine acted compared to the human.
- One interview was cancelled 30 minutes before the start because of a doctor's appointment. That's understandable. The rescheduling link didn't work, so I chased the recruiter for a few days. When we finally met, the conversation opened with "tell me about yourself" and no other warm-up. It was planned for 45 minutes and ended after 15. I was rejected three days later and I consider it a bullet dodged.
- Another recruiter and I both sat alone in separate Teams rooms for a while, because Teams helpfully created two meetings. The earliest rescheduled slot was three weeks away. This is the same company that later told me my salary expectations didn't fit, so it was a lot of calendar for a small outcome.

## Ghosting, in styles

Over two months I started to see that ghosting has types.

- **The honest ghost.** An automated email after a referral: if you haven't heard from us in eight weeks, we've gone with other candidates. At least it's clear from the start.
- **The permanent "processing".** A referral to a bank. Two months later the status is still "being processed". I'd love to know what they're processing.
- **The "in review" ghost.** Similar, except it's at a chip company and comes after a rejection from a different team at the same company. I've been "in review" for over a month now.
- **The "good fit, we'll call you" ghost.** Two one-hour calls with a recruiter, because one hour apparently isn't enough to ask what needs to be asked. Then "you're a good fit, we'll get back to you in a week". That was the last I heard.
- **The six-interview ghost.** The most elaborate case. Six rounds, with recruiters, directors and senior engineers in various combinations. They shared the compensation structure and the equity plan, told me they really wanted me, and said I'd get an offer within a week. After a week I asked what was happening. I was told they were finalizing their regional priorities for next year. Two weeks later, they apologized for the silence and explained that headcount was under discussion, with a decision hoped for in December. I don't know what to think about this. I'd also like to say that I've never been so well informed about a company's budget cycle.

## Salary bingo

I tell every recruiter my minimum salary in the first call. It's the one thing I can do to save everyone's time. It didn't work, and the consultancy above wasn't the only one.

- A company I had given my minimum came back after three interviews with an offer far below it. I'm fairly sure I said the number out loud, so I wonder where they thought it went.
- Two more companies went through three interviews each, everyone happy, and then rejected me because their maximum was far below my stated minimum. Why spend everyone's time? I don't know.
- One more company heard my minimum and made an offer that was a bit below it. That one at least sat in the same neighborhood, and it came with an actual offer, so I'll count it as progress.

## Fast, and also fast

Two companies stand out because they actually made offers, and their processes could hardly have looked more different.

The first said right away that they care about a fast process. The application went in on September 8. The first reply came on October 1, the first interview on October 23, and the probation day on November 17. That is a quick process in the sense that a glacier is quick.

The second had a recruiter call within five days, a hiring manager the next day, a peer after that, and a technical challenge with a presentation. They told me that was the final interview, then changed their minds and added a director, and then an in-person meeting. It was longer than I'd expect, but at every step a human was talking to me, and I knew where I stood.

Both turned into offers. I'm not sure which of them was faster, but I know which one felt like it.

## What I'd say to a friend

I'm not going to turn this into a lessons list, because I'm still very much in the middle of the process. But two things kept proving true.

**A referral gets you through the door, not through the process.** Most of my referrals led to a conversation with a human, which is worth a lot when the alternative is a pile of applications where nobody looks. But a few led nowhere, and none of them changed what happened after that. The Munich story, the missing interviewers and the vanished recruiters all came with a referral attached.

**The more human the process, the better it went.** The companies that gave me a person to talk to, a clear next step and an honest answer, even when the answer was a no, were also the ones that made offers. The ones that ran me through the most automation, from the chatbot to the unattended tests, taught me the least about the job and about whether I'd like it.

The CV, as it turns out, was the easy part. It still builds on every push without complaint. If only the rest of the process had a pipeline.
