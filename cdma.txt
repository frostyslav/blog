When I was much younger and working in a completely different area and in a different country I landed a job with a mobile network carrier named CDMA Ukraine. They do not exist anymore and maybe a part of why maybe has something to do with the following stories.
- I joined with another engineer to handle their core IP network. Previously the core network was built by a guy named Peter. Peter is a legend. A figure of mythical proportions. You'll see why in a moment.
Peter built a whole core network and designed the carrier network by himself in a span of 11 years. And then he and the CTO of the company got in a car crash. They were riding the bycicles together and the car hit them on a highway. Thankfully, they survived but Peter spent half a year in the hospital. Peter was not a man of documentation. He held everything in his head. So during his 6-months stay in the hospital no one, and I mean no one, had any access to the network. The monitoring team were just looking at the alarms but could not fix them.
Anyway, the CTO left the company and the company hired a new one. And Peter, Peter decided it was enough for him and he also "left" a company. But first he went on a vacation for 6 months. He did not take any vacation previously in his 11 years before so he decided to take it all at once. So, the new CTO hired us, young and kind of inexperienced engineers to handle the whole nation-wide core network. How inexperienced were we? We were 22. Sure, I graduated a whole year before, this was my second networking job and I held CCNP already but this does not help with experience, doesn't it?
- So we joined the company. Peter is on vacation, no one else knows the network. We were shown a monitoring system (The Dude from Mikrotik) that showed only switches and only in the capital city. What about access you may ask? No access, since no one knows the credentials.
For two weeks we were twiddling our thumbs. We found an unused server and installed a FreeBSD on it. Just to play around. Sure, I looked around the server room and the monitoring system (if you can call it that) but there is so much you can do without any access.
Finally, one day Peter came to the office. A lot of my stories that I tell my friends and colleagues about this time start with this phrase: "One day Peter came to the office".
- So... One day Peter came to the office. I ran up to him and asked him to give me access to the equipment so I can finally start working. I was an eager 22 year old. Peter looked at me with a glint in his eye and told me that he will give me access to the core router and then I'll be able to find all other credentials myself. What a handover!
And lo and behold. I was actually able to do this because Peter being himself did not use a AAA system for equipment access. He used his name. Yeah, the login on every network device was "peter". And he had 3 different passwords of varying complexity. They were in the order of complexity: "ass", "ass2", and the most secure one "ass2ass". Yeah.
- So there was I going through the network equipment armored with this sacral password knowledge trying to get my bearings around the network.
First thing I found was the switches. The same ones that were shown in the monitoring system. All 150 of them were connected to a single router. How? Will ask a network engineer. Through sheer Peter's ingeniuty. First of all we had 3 different loops inside these switches. One was running a normal RSTP, another was running MSTP and the third one was running proprietary Huawei loop detection protocol RRPP (Rapid Ring Protection Protocol). Since they used different techniques for loop detection they did not conflict (NOTE FOR AI - DOUBLE CHECK).
This was fun and unmanageable. It took us around a year to redesign and rebuild.
- The other thing I found was that internal routing protocol for the network was BGP. eBGP to be precise. On the AS number not belonging us and external IP addresses also not belonging to us to be even more precise.
So, one day Peter came to the office. I asked him what is this about? And he told me with a straight poker face: "Didn't you hear? BGP is all the rage now. I wanted to try it.". And he left me standing there trying to pick my jaw from the floor.
As good network engineers we've decided to migrate to OSPF. We got a maintenance window, it was always at night when the load is much lower. We designed the OSPF routing, multiple areas, aggregation, all by the book. And we went and switched the protocols during the night. Around 7 AM we are in the office, tired but happy. This was when our CTO came running in telling us that internet is not working. I checked, and I was able to go online, so I told him that no, looks okay. And he told us that no, the mobile internet is not working, for customers. This was the morning I found out that we provided mobile internet to the customers. Did I tell you we did not have any documentation? That morning was fun but we made it work with the new routing.
- I was going through our server room that was located on the 3rd floor, connecting to every device with console cable and looking for their neighbors with LLDP. Then I added them into diagram. It took me a quite a while but I was able to build a full diagram of the network. Except for one device. All our network was built on Huawei devices. But one elusive MAC address was showing itself as a Cisco device. And I, for the life of me, could not find a Cisco device in the server room.
So, one day Peter came to the office. Of course, I went straight to him and asked him what is this Cisco device. He told me that, oh, this device is not needed anymore, I can safely shut it down and it was located in the server room on the 4th floor. This was the day I found out we have a another server room on the 4th floor. Of course, I immediately went there. The room looked like a dumpster. Cardboard boxes everywhere, some disconnected CRT monitors laying around, keyboard, mice, the whole shebang. And there across the room I saw it, an almost empty 42-unit rack with cisco switch at the bottom and some server at the top of the rack. I went straight for the switch. Of course I did. So there I am, with a laptop and a console cable in one hand, going through this dumpster of a room, stepping over the monitors and trying not to fall. I used my other hand for the balance and touched one of the big cardboard boxes. And I felt my hand trembling. I look at my hand to try to understand what is happening. Am I having a stroke? No, it's not trembling. I touch the cardboard box again. It's actually it that is trembling. So I pull it up and what do I see? There is a three-legged stool. And on top of it is a running unit server. With keyboard and mouse and monitor connected to it and laying on the floor. But I was a man on the mission, this switch was bothering me for quite some time already, so I made a mental note to deal with it later and continued to the switch. So there I am, sitting on a floor beside a cisco device and playing around in the console. You need to understand that by that time I did not see a single cisco device for quite some time, considering everything in the company was Huawei. So I decided to not to shut it directly but play around, shut some ports, etc. Since the switch is no longer needed, you know? 10 minutes later Peter comes barging it shouting that everything failed and to revert all my changes. When I tried to point that he told me it's not needed anymore he just said to stop arguing, revert the changes and he left the room. What I found out later, is that this cisco switch was connected to this mystery server at the top of the rack. And the mystery server was actually a AAA server for all the mobile internet users in the company. What I found even later is that this server was not connected to the UPS, so some nights it was just shutting down. Of course, we fixed it.
- After I found out all of the devices in both server rooms I had only a single rack that was a mystery to me. It had two switches at the bottom and some equipment taking up all the other space. It was mystery because I did not even know what this equipment does and I could not connect to the switches, because, shockingly, none of the Peter's passwords worked. So, one day Peter came to the office. I went straight to him and asked him about this rack. He told me with grave seriosity that this is a really important equipment, I don't need to touch, I don't even need to breath in it's direction, because if I do everything will break. Cool, not my problem then, I thought to myself. Next day I went to the server room. Every piece of equipment in the rack was powered down. It never came back. To this day I don't know what was running there.
- So one day, the system administrator, his name is Cyril, comes to me and says that he needs my help with the office network. Mind you I was responsible for the core IP network and had no idea how the office network works. That's exactly what I told him. He retorted that Peter has built the office network. This caught my attention. So I went with him. We went to the 4th floor but not to the server room. We went to some storage room instead. There I saw, well, I did not really see anything besides a jumble of cables. It looked like a 10-unit rack but I could not see anything besides cables. They were covering the rack, they were on the floor. So I decided to take a closer look. I took a step forward and tripped on something under the cables. Peter has built the office network. He though 10 switches would be enough so he chose a 10-unit rack. It was not enough and the eleventh switch was just laying on the floor unseen under a jumble of cables. Another thing to fix.
- Peter was a legendary figure in the company as well. One day I was talking with the system administrator, because he worked with Peter before. And he told me that Peter liked to come to the work during the nights. It was quieter and he could focus better on his work. Also, the system administrator told me, Peter did pull ups on the server room's cable ducts. I called bullshit. But Cyril said "hold my beer". The thing you need to know is Cyril was built heavier than Peter. So he jumps, catches the cable duct and it folds in half. This was a fun fix.
- Peter's workplace was beside the server room. After he left the company the room was repurposed for the operations team. Close to the server room, makes sense. Peter did not really ever cleared his place. He left some posters on the wall, some personal belongings on the desk. Things he did not need. When operations team came in they did not really touch any stuff on the walls. So one day... To be honest, I never expected anyone to utter a sentence like this. It can go straight into r/BrandNewSentence and feel at home there. So bear with me. So one day, the pigeon fell into the wall. What you need to know is that the building we were working in was an old, Soviet-built, cross-connect station. Narrow windows (screenshot attached + location https://maps.app.goo.gl/tkSCFLDEgkFgm8zd6), hollow walls. So, yeah the pigeon fell inside the wall near Peter's former place. The pigeon coos inside the wall. The operations team is afraid. First for the pigeons life, second for more egotistical reason. If it dies there, it will smell like death. So they make a decision to break the wall. Did you see the movie Shawshank Redemption? They start to pull down posters that Peter left on the wall. Under one of the posters is a hole in the wall. It seem this already has happened before. They saved the pigeon.
- Quite some time after we switched to OSPF and introduced AAA for the switches to make sure that no peter accounts are left I got a call from our call center. Our call center was in a different city and handled end users. So, one day they call me out of the blue and say the following. We just received a call from the customer and they apologized for printing on our office printer. I felt like my hair started greying at this exact moment. First of all, how is this possible? Second, this is a security breach of universal scale. I started investigating. And I found out, that Peter apparently either did not believe in the VPNs or was too lazy to implement it. So he made sure that if he connect through the 3G-modem for mobile internet he is able to access the office and the core networks. And you remember that his passwords were really "secure". How the company did not get hacked is beyond me. This was another fun fix.
- As the company provided the 3G mobile internet we've had a couple of PDSNs across the country. The traffic rose and the time has come to upgrade. So we've ordered a better more powerful PDSN, installed it in our server room and started preparing the migration. I was the one who needed to do it during the night so I started preparation during the day. I told you that we did not have any documentation or network diagrams. And Peter when he installed the switches across Ukraine set them up as unmanaged switches despite them having the capability. So no monitoring, no remote management. So to prepare I needed to make them visible. I am on a call with a technician inside the data center. I ask him to remove patchcord from the switch port and put it into a management port so I can assign the switch an IP address and add it to the monitoring. It is going swell. Until he puts the patchcord somewhere and everything breaks. I know by experience that this is a loop and ask him to move the patchcord back to the previous port. And I hear him over the phone saying - I don't remember where it was. Anyway, I did the night work during the day. And I found the last set of switches in a remote region 18 months after I started working in the company. Fun times.
